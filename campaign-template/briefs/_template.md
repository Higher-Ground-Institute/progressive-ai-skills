## The short version

Three to five sentences. What is happening, who decided it, what it costs, what happens next
and when.

## How we got here

Chronological, sourced, dated. Ordinance numbers, docket numbers, vote counts, meeting dates.
Link the primary record every time one exists — the actual minutes, not the article about the
minutes.

| Date | What happened | Vote | Record |
|---|---|---|---|
| | | | |

## The numbers

The dollar figures, rates, counts, and comparisons, each sourced. A table beats a paragraph
here.

Compare this jurisdiction with its neighbors, its own past, or the state average—whichever
comparison is honest and available.

## Who decides what happens next

The body, the members, the next meeting date, how public comment works, and how a resident
actually participates. Include the address and the sign-up deadline.

This is the part that makes the brief useful to a non-supporter, and it costs twenty minutes
of research.

## The arguments

State each side as its strongest advocate would state it. If the campaign disagrees with a
position, quote a real person making it well rather than a straw version.

**The case for [X]:**

**The case against [X]:**

**What both sides agree on:**

## Where [CANDIDATE] stands

Clearly separated from the reference material above, and short. The brief earns trust by
being useful first. If this section is longer than the rest, the document has become a
mailer.

If the topic is on the `no-position-yet` list in `positioning.md`, this section says so and
names the open question. It does not guess.

## What is still unknown

Open questions, unreleased records, pending studies, FOIA requests outstanding.
For live decisions, add `[FOLLOW UP YYYY-MM-DD — verify <decision or record> and update this brief]`.
