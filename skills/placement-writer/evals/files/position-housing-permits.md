# Why has Ashfield County approved 3,100 homes and built 210?

Because a residential building permit that state law says should take 45 days is taking 287,
and the county has run the same four plan reviewers since 2014 while permit volume rose 190%.
I would fund three more reviewers and a tracking system in the FY2027 budget and publish the
turnaround number every month.

## Why

Between 2021 and 2025 the Ashfield County Board of Commissioners approved 3,100 units of
housing. As of the March 12 board meeting, 210 had been completed
([minutes](https://ashfieldcounty.example.gov/minutes/2026-03-12), retrieved 2026-06-10).

That gap does not come from zoning. Every one of those 3,100 units is already entitled — the
Board voted yes, the land use is settled, the fight most counties are having was already had
here and housing won. The units are stuck one step later, in a permitting office that cannot
keep up with the approvals the Board keeps handing it.

The number that explains it is 287. That is the median turnaround, in days, for a residential
building permit in Ashfield County in the first quarter of 2026. The statutory target is 45
days. In 2019 the county was hitting it ([Development Services Quarterly Report
Q1-2026](https://devservices.ashfieldcounty.example.gov/reports/2026-q1), retrieved
2026-06-10). The county publishes this figure in an appendix to a quarterly PDF and has never
said anything about it.

Two hundred and eighty-seven days is not a delay. It is a construction season. A builder who
files in February does not break ground until the following winter, which means carrying the
land, the loan, and the crew through a year of nothing. Small builders cannot absorb that, so
they stop filing here. The ones who remain price the delay into the house.

The staffing is the direct cause. Development Services has four budgeted plan reviewer
positions. It had four in 2014. Permit volume is up 190% over the same period ([FY2026 adopted
budget](https://ashfieldcounty.example.gov/budget/fy2026), p. 143, retrieved 2026-06-10). No
process survives that.

The comparison that matters is next door. Calder County has a comparable population and a
comparable permit volume, runs nine plan reviewers, and posts a 61-day median ([Calder County
Development Services 2025 annual report](https://calder.example.gov/ds/2025-annual), retrieved
2026-06-11). Same state, same building code, same contractors driving between the two. The
difference is five people.

I spent eleven years inspecting residential projects for this county — more than 1,400 of
them, from 2013 to 2024. I have read the file on a good share of the 2,890 units that have
not been built. They are not exotic. They are duplexes and single-family infill waiting on a
plan review that nobody has been assigned to.

The public noticed before the county did. Forty-one residents signed up for public comment at
the March 12 board meeting about the Millbrook Commons delay alone
([minutes](https://ashfieldcounty.example.gov/minutes/2026-03-12), retrieved 2026-06-10).
That is not a normal turnout for a procedural item.

## What I would do

Three things, all of them at the Board's FY2027 budget markup in April 2027.

**Fund three plan reviewer positions.** Three at $118,000 fully loaded is $354,000. That does
not get us to Calder's nine, but it gets us to seven, and the 2023 workload study put the
right number somewhere between six and nine.

**Fund a permit tracking system, $58,000.** Right now a builder cannot find out where their
application sits without calling and asking someone to walk down a hall. Every neighboring
county has a portal.

**Publish the median turnaround every month, on the county website, on the first business
day.** This one is free and it is the one that actually holds. A number nobody publishes is a
number nobody has to answer for.

Total is $412,000. The county's unallocated general fund balance was $2.1 million in the
FY2026 adopted budget (p. 12). This is affordable and it is not close.

## What I don't know yet

Whether three reviewers is the right number. The 2023 workload study recommended a range and
the county has not re-run the analysis since permit volume changed again. I would commission
it in the first quarter, and if it comes back saying we need five, I would rather say that
than defend a number I picked because it sounded modest.

I also do not know how much of the 287 days is review time versus applications sitting in a
queue before anyone opens them. The county does not break that out. Those two problems have
different fixes — the first is staffing, the second is intake triage — and the tracking system
is how we find out which one we have.

The strongest objection is that adding staff to a slow office adds slow staff. That is fair,
and it is why the published monthly number matters more than the headcount. If turnaround has
not moved after two budget cycles, the problem is process rather than people, and the county
should have to say so in public.
